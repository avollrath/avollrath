**TalentAdore** is a Finnish recruitment SaaS platform. The desktop product had grown into something fairly complex — a full ATS with candidate pipelines, messaging, job management, analytics — and the question was how to bring the core of that to mobile in a way that was actually useful rather than just a shrunken version of the desktop UI.

## The design challenge

Mobile recruitment tools have a specific use case: a hiring manager or recruiter who's away from their desk and needs to act on something. Review a candidate, leave a note, move someone forward, check the pipeline status. That's a narrower job than the full desktop product, and designing for it meant making deliberate cuts — deciding what was essential for that context and what could wait until you were back at a computer.

I designed the interface from scratch in **Adobe XD**, building out the full navigation structure, key screens, and interaction flows. The prototype was interactive enough that the product and engineering teams could click through the core journeys without needing a demo — they could feel the pacing themselves.

## Mobile scope

The project also required consistency with an existing product ecosystem. A mobile concept can introduce new patterns, but it still needs to feel like the same product family. Colors, status labels, candidate terminology, and interaction language all had to connect back to the desktop experience.

Recruitment workflows also involve sensitive information, so the interface needed to feel calm and deliberate. A mobile UI can encourage speed, but it should not make consequential actions feel casual. Candidate movement, notes, and communication flows all benefit from clear confirmation patterns and enough context to prevent mistakes.

Designing this also clarified how different mobile SaaS work is from consumer mobile design. The goal is not delight for its own sake; it is confidence under time pressure. Buttons, empty states, and confirmation flows all have to make actions feel safe because moving a candidate or sending a message has real workflow consequences.

The prototype helped because it made tradeoffs visible early. Static screens can look convincing while hiding awkward transitions. A clickable flow exposes whether navigation feels natural, whether a button is in the right place, and whether a user can recover after moving into a detail view. That made feedback from product and engineering more concrete.

For me, this project was a strong example of design as product reduction. The work was not to make the desktop product smaller; it was to identify the mobile job and design a tool around that job.

The most important design decision was what not to include. The desktop ATS can support deep configuration, bulk actions, reporting, and complex candidate workflows. A mobile app should not try to mirror all of that. The strongest mobile use cases are review, triage, communication, and quick decisions. Designing around those moments kept the interface focused.

That meant prioritising candidate cards, pipeline movement, message context, and clear status indicators. Dense desktop tables had to become focused mobile views. Secondary information moved behind progressive disclosure so the first screen could answer the question a recruiter is most likely asking: what needs my attention right now?

## From prototype to product

Once development started I stayed involved to keep the build aligned with the design intent. The gap between a prototype and a shipped product is where a lot of design decisions get quietly reversed — not out of laziness, but because implementation surfaces constraints that weren't visible in XD. Being available to make fast decisions about those tradeoffs kept the final product closer to what had been designed.

It's a part of the process I find genuinely interesting: the negotiation between what was intended and what's actually buildable under real constraints.
